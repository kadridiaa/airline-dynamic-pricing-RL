\section{Introduction}
An airline sells the seats of a flight over 30 days and sets a price every day.
With a known demand model, Dynamic Programming (DP) computes the optimal policy; in practice the model is estimated, hence wrong, and planning with a wrong model is sub-optimal~\cite[\S 8.3]{sutton}.
Q-learning needs no model but many episodes.
\textbf{Research question:} from which model error does learning without a model outperform planning with a wrong one, for a given budget of episodes?

\section{Problem formulation}
\textbf{MDP.} State $s=(c,t)$: $c\in\{0..10\}$ seats left, $t\in\{0..30\}$ days to departure (keeping $t$ in the state makes it Markov).
Actions: six prices $p\in\{50,80,\dots,200\}$\,\euro.
Selling $k$ seats gives $r=p\,k$ and $(c,t)\to(c-k,t-1)$; episodes end at $t=0$ or $c=0$; $\gamma=1$.
This gives 341 states and 2\,046 Q-values.

\textbf{Demand.} Leisure customers arrive at rate $\lambda_L=0.8$/day ($0.3$ when $t\le10$), business customers at $\lambda_B=0.1$ ($1.0$ when $t\le10$).
Willingness to pay (WTP) is exponential with mean $m_L=80$\,\euro, $m_B=180$\,\euro, so a customer buys with probability $e^{-p/m}$.
By Poisson thinning, demand is $D\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\mu)$, $\mu=\lambda_L e^{-p/m_L}+\lambda_B e^{-p/m_B}$, and sales $k=\min(D,c)$.
We use $C=10$ seats: with 20, the plane almost never sells out and a myopic policy loses only 0.1\,\%, against 18.9\,\% with 10 (load factor 77\,\%).

\section{Methods}
\textbf{DP.} Bellman optimality, $V^*(c,t)=\max_a \sum_{k} P(k\mid c,t,a)\,[p_a k+\gamma V^*(c-k,t-1)]$, with $V^*(c,0)=V^*(0,t)=0$.
As $V^*(\cdot,t)$ only depends on $V^*(\cdot,t-1)$, one backward sweep is exact.
\textbf{Model error.} The DP plans with both WTP means multiplied by $(1+x)$, $x\in[-50,+50]\,\%$ (an error on $\lambda$ costs at most 5\,\%).
\textbf{Q-learning, SARSA.} Tabular, $Q=0$, $\varepsilon$-greedy with $\varepsilon$ decaying exponentially from 1 to 0.01 over the episode budget; $\alpha_n = 0.5/(1+40\,n/N)$ at episode $n$ of a budget of $N$ (a constant $\alpha$ does not converge in this stochastic environment: Q-values keep oscillating and the greedy action flips). These values were selected among 9 configurations by the best worst case over seeds. Both methods only interact with the simulator, never with the demand model.
\textbf{Baselines.} Best fixed price; best linearly rising price.
\textbf{Evaluation.} All policies are scored on the \emph{true} model by exact policy evaluation (cross-checked with 10\,000 simulated flights), as a percentage of the optimal revenue; learning methods use 10 seeds and are reported with 95\,\% confidence intervals of the mean (Student).
Checks: the DP value lies in the 95\,\% confidence interval of its simulated revenue; no wrong-model DP or baseline beats it.

\textbf{Optimal policy} (Fig.~\ref{fig:policy}). The price never increases with the seats left, as predicted by~\cite{gallego}; it jumps when business customers arrive, and drops to 80\,\euro{} at $t=11$--$12$ to sell remaining seats to leisure customers.
A rising price already reaches 97.7\,\% (Table~\ref{tab:main}), consistent with the near-optimality of simple pricing heuristics~\cite{gallego}.

\textbf{E2: tipping point between planning and learning} (Fig.~\ref{fig:e2}). The DP stays above 97\,\% for $|x|\le20\,\%$.
Errors are asymmetric: underestimating the WTP ($x=-50\,\%$) drops DP to 75.8\,\% because seats sell out too cheaply, against 92.9\,\% at $+50\,\%$.
Model-free RL therefore beats the wrong DP, significantly at the 95\,\% level, with $1\,000$ episodes at $x=-50\,\%$ and $10\,000$ at $-40\,\%$ and $-30\,\%$.
When the WTP is overestimated, the DP is more resilient: RL only wins at $+40\,\%$ ($30\,000$ episodes) and $+50\,\%$ ($10\,000$). For $-20\,\%\le x\le+30\,\%$, the DP wins or ties at every budget.
With 66 tests at the 95\,\% level, a few isolated cells may be significant by chance; our conclusions rest on the large contiguous regions, not on single cells.

\textbf{E4: discount factor} (Fig.~\ref{fig:e4}). A DP optimising a discounted criterion sells seats too early: with $\gamma=0.5$ it sells 6.8 seats before business customers arrive (4.1 with $\gamma=1$) and earns 83.7\,\% of the optimum (97.8\,\% with $\gamma=0.9$).
Q-learning (30\,000 episodes, 10 seeds) shows the same myopia: 95.6\,\% ($\gamma=1$), 89.7\,\% ($\gamma=0.9$) and 74.7\,\% ($\gamma=0.5$), selling 7.1 seats before business customers arrive.

\textbf{E1 \& E3: learning budget and policy heatmaps} (Figs.~\ref{fig:e1} and~\ref{fig:e3}). Q-learning reaches 95\,\% of the optimum from 30\,000 episodes (95.6\,\%, CI [95.3, 96.0]), and both methods plateau at 96.4\,\% at 100\,000 episodes, significantly below the rising-price heuristic (97.7\,\%; upper CI bound 97.0\,\%).
Off-policy Q-learning converges faster at intermediate budgets (93.6\,\% vs 91.0\,\% at 10k), while SARSA has lower variance at convergence (0.3 vs 0.7\,\%).
Heatmaps show that RL captures the structure of the optimal policy; 21 of the 300 states (few seats left very early) are never learned because they are almost never reached, and rarely visited states remain noisy at little cost in revenue.

\section{Conclusion}
Learning outperforms planning only for large model errors: an under-estimation of the WTP by 30\,\% or more (with $10^3$ to $10^4$ flights), or an over-estimation by 40\,\% or more ($10^4$ to $3\cdot10^4$ flights). For moderate errors ($-20\,\%\le x\le+30\,\%$) or scarce data, planning with an imperfect model beats model-free learning.
Even with $10^5$ flights, tabular RL stays below a simple rising-price rule tuned on the true model: the value of planning (or of a good model) lies in the last few percent.
\textbf{Limitations and perspective.} One episode is one flight: $30\,000$ episodes would be about 82 years of daily flights, so in practice RL itself would need a simulator, i.e.\ a model; this strengthens the case for planning.
The RL hyper-parameters were tuned by scoring on the true model, which favours RL; tuning on independent seeds or a held-out model would only make RL weaker.
Demand is synthetic, only the WTP is perturbed, and methods are tabular: realistic aircraft would require value function approximation, and learning the model from data (model-based RL, Dyna~\cite[ch.~8]{sutton}) is the natural next step between our two extremes.
