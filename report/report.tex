\section{Introduction}
An airline sells the seats of a flight over 30 days and sets a price every day.
With a known demand model, Dynamic Programming (DP) computes the optimal policy; in practice the model is estimated, hence wrong, and planning with a wrong model is sub-optimal~\cite[\S 8.3]{sutton}.
Q-learning needs no model but many episodes.
\textbf{Research question:} from which model error does learning without a model outperform planning with a wrong one, for a given budget of episodes?

\section{Problem formulation}
\textbf{MDP.} State $s=(c,t)$: $c\in\{0..10\}$ seats left, $t\in\{0..30\}$ days to departure (keeping $t$ in the state makes it Markov).
Actions: six prices $p\in\{50,80,\dots,200\}$\,\euro.
Selling $k$ seats gives $r=p\,k$ and $(c,t)\to(c-k,t-1)$; episodes end at $t=0$ or $c=0$; $\gamma=1$.
This gives 341 states and 2\,046 Q-values.

\textbf{Demand.} Leisure customers arrive at rate $\lambda_L=0.8$/day ($0.3$ when $t\le10$), business customers at $\lambda_B=0.1$ ($1.0$ when $t\le10$).
Willingness to pay (WTP) is exponential with mean $m_L=80$\,\euro, $m_B=180$\,\euro, so a customer buys with probability $e^{-p/m}$.
By Poisson thinning, demand is $D\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\mu)$, $\mu=\lambda_L e^{-p/m_L}+\lambda_B e^{-p/m_B}$, and sales $k=\min(D,c)$.
We use $C=10$ seats: with 20, the plane almost never sells out and a myopic policy loses only 0.1\,\%, against 18.9\,\% with 10 (load factor 77\,\%).

\section{Methods}
\textbf{DP.} Bellman optimality, $V^*(c,t)=\max_a \sum_{k} P(k\mid c,t,a)\,[p_a k+\gamma V^*(c-k,t-1)]$, with $V^*(c,0)=V^*(0,t)=0$.
As $V^*(\cdot,t)$ only depends on $V^*(\cdot,t-1)$, one backward sweep is exact.
\textbf{Model error.} The DP plans with both WTP means multiplied by $(1+x)$, $x\in[-50,+50]\,\%$ (an error on $\lambda$ costs at most 5\,\%).
\textbf{Q-learning, SARSA.} Tabular, $Q=0$, $\varepsilon$-greedy with $\varepsilon$ decaying from 1; they only interact with the simulator. \todo{final $\alpha$, $\varepsilon$ schedule}
\textbf{Baselines.} Best fixed price; best linearly rising price.
\textbf{Evaluation.} All policies are scored on the \emph{true} model by exact policy evaluation (cross-checked with 10\,000 simulated flights), as a percentage of the optimal revenue; learning methods use 10 seeds.
Checks: the DP value lies in the 95\,\% confidence interval of its simulated revenue; no wrong-model DP or baseline beats it.

\textbf{Optimal policy} (Fig.~\ref{fig:policy}). The price never increases with the seats left, as predicted by~\cite{gallego}; it jumps when business customers arrive, and drops to 80\,\euro{} at $t=11$--$12$ to sell remaining seats to leisure customers.
A rising price already reaches 97.7\,\% (Table~\ref{tab:main}), consistent with the near-optimality of simple pricing heuristics~\cite{gallego}.

\textbf{E2: planning with a wrong model} (Fig.~\ref{fig:e2}). The DP stays above 97\,\% for $|x|\le20\,\%$.
Errors are asymmetric: underestimating the WTP ($x=-50\,\%$) gives 75.8\,\% because seats sell out too cheaply (88\,\% of flights full), against 92.9\,\% at $+50\,\%$.
For the same wrong model, the DP always beats the rising-price heuristic.
